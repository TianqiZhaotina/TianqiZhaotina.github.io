RESEARCH INTERESTS

Student agency in human–AI learning

I am interested in how AI-supported K–12 STEM and computing environments can help students become more self-directed learners. Rather than defining agency by how much work students complete without AI, I ask where meaningful decisions, judgments, and responsibility remain throughout human–AI collaboration.

RESEARCH AGENDA

From using AI to remaining in charge

A polished final product can conceal very different learning processes. I focus on the moments when students shape the work, make choices about AI, and take responsibility for what they create.

01

Goal & Ideation

Who sets the direction, develops the core idea, and defines what success should look like?

02

Planning & Delegation

How do students organize the work and decide what to do themselves or delegate to AI?

03

Evaluation & Justification

How do students test AI contributions, revise or reject them, and explain their decisions?

Design learning environments that cultivate agency, not only efficiency.

Conceptualize how decision authority is delegated, shared, and reclaimed.

Trace agency through chats, screen actions, revisions, reflections, and transfer tasks.

My longer-term goal is to design AI-rich learning environments that do more than preserve human ownership. I hope they can help students become self-directed, high-agency learners who use AI strategically while remaining responsible for their own learning trajectories.

LEARNING ANALYTICS · HUMAN–AI CO-DESIGN

Research question formulation with AI

Research Associate · Learning Analytics Lab · Teachers College, Columbia University · with Yasemin Gulbahar · Dec 2025–Jun 2026

This study examined how novice learning analytics researchers formulate and refine research questions with language models. Rather than treating a better final prompt as the whole story, I studied how people iterate, check constraints, operationalize constructs, question AI suggestions, and recover from methodological problems.

EXPERIMENT & METHODOLOGY

Following both the product and the process

The product rubric examined specificity, data–method alignment, theoretical depth, and inquiry coherence. The process rubric examined prompt iteration, critical evaluation, help-seeking, and challenge resolution.

  1. 01
    Study design

    An IRB-approved, within-subjects think-aloud study with six master’s students. Each participant worked through two learning analytics scenarios with two language models, producing 24 sessions in total.

  2. 02
    Learning task

    Participants developed a Research Inquiry Cluster: one primary research question, two sub-questions, and a short methodological rationale grounded in the data available in each scenario.

  3. 03
    Evidence collected

    Chat histories, think-aloud data, final inquiry clusters, and reflections captured both the quality of the final research design and the interaction process that produced it.

  4. 04
    Analysis

    I combined product and process rubrics with hybrid deductive–inductive coding. The interaction data were segmented into 129 goal-centered episodes and reviewed using documented coding decisions.

RESEARCH OUTPUT

A practice-based roadmap

By connecting final inquiry quality with the interaction paths that produced it, the study develops practical guidance for research methods training: how to make constraints explicit, translate abstract constructs into observable indicators, verify method–data fit, and retain researcher judgment when AI proposes an attractive but infeasible direction.

First-authored AERA 2027 individual paper proposal: Human–AI Co-thinking in Research Question Formulation as Methodological Apprenticeship · under review.

UNDERGRADUATE DISSERTATION · OCT 2023–JUN 2024

Teaching Excellence Framework and lecturer practice

University of Manchester

I designed and conducted a qualitative dissertation study to examine how university lecturers interpreted the Teaching Excellence Framework and whether institutional accountability measures translated into day-to-day teaching practice.

The project focused on lecturers’ perspectives rather than treating institutional metrics as a direct proxy for teaching quality. This made it possible to compare the policy’s intended influence with the decisions educators described making in their own classrooms.

RESEARCH DESIGN & DATA COLLECTION

Moving from policy claims to lecturers’ lived accounts

The design connected a policy-level question to evidence from practice. Semi-structured interviews provided a shared frame across participants while preserving room for unexpected examples, tensions, and critical perspectives to emerge.

  1. 01
    Research framing

    I translated a broad policy question into a study of lecturers’ own accounts: how they understood TEF, whether it affected their motivation and pedagogical decisions, and what they considered credible evidence of teaching quality.

  2. 02
    Cross-disciplinary sample

    The study included five University of Manchester lecturers working across Education, Climate Change, Circular Economy, and Fashion Management, allowing the analysis to examine both shared concerns and differences across teaching contexts.

  3. 03
    Data collection

    I developed and used a semi-structured interview protocol. A consistent set of core questions made responses comparable, while open follow-up questions allowed lecturers to explain concrete experiences with feedback, accountability, and classroom practice.

  4. 04
    Qualitative analysis

    I worked from the interview transcripts, coded the responses, compared patterns across participants, and organized the evidence into four thematic areas linking policy interpretation with motivation, teaching decisions, and perceptions of quality.

FINDINGS

Limited direct influence, with important tensions

Participants described limited direct influence of TEF on classroom practice and mixed views on whether feedback metrics meaningfully supported improvement. Their accounts also raised concerns about metric-based accountability, bias in student evaluations, and the marketization of teaching quality.

RESEARCH OUTPUT

A complete qualitative dissertation

The final output brought together the research questions, literature and policy context, interview protocol, qualitative dataset, four-theme analytic framework, findings, and implications in an undergraduate dissertation.
